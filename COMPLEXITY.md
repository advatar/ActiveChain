Honest answer: roughly half of it does, and I can be specific about which half.

The part that's genuinely forced

Some complexity is a faithful consequence of the goals, not a failure of restraint.

Committing to post-quantum at genesis is one decision that generates a cascade you can't escape: ML-DSA signatures are ~15–40× larger than Ed25519 and don't aggregate like BLS, which forces witness/intent separation, certificate-only consensus over batch roots, short-lived witnesses rather than permanent history. CASH.md §§4–7 didn't invent that complexity; it discovered it. Same with proof-carrying validity — once validity must be checkable without re-execution, you need a proving system, and once it must be PQ, you're in PQVM.md territory.

And the authorization kernel — principals, credentials, capabilities, APL — is the part that reduces total system complexity. It's more consensus surface in exchange for every application above it not reinventing identity, delegation, and policy inconsistently. ARCHITECTURE_GUIDE.md §2 states that trade explicitly and it's the right one.

The local engineering discipline is also genuinely complexity-reducing: no_std, forbid(unsafe_code), total functions, bounded everything, default deny, fixed metering independent of predicate truth. Each crate is small and provable. That's not where the problem is.

The part that isn't

Count the independent planes: authorization kernel, ObjectVM, a separate cash plane, shielded pool, PQ proving VM, DA, compute/AI jobs, protected ordering, credential/ZK pipeline, regulated compliance profiles, multi-asset tokenization, payment connectors, mobile wallets, agent enrollment, dBrowser integration. Fifteen or so, each of which is a product elsewhere.

Two of those are worth looking at hard, because they're complexity that was generated by other complexity rather than by a goal:

The cash plane exists because the general VM is too heavy for payments. So there's now a second execution path with its own fee market, its own AIR, its own state partitioning, its own proof profile, its own benchmark harness. When a general mechanism needs a special case carved out of it for the most common transaction, that usually means the general mechanism is mis-specified — or that payments should never have been routed through it. Either reading suggests something upstream is wrong, and the fix is upstream, not a second lane.

The purpose-specific PQ STARK VM was adopted as a side effect. PQVM.md reaches it by elimination: RISC Zero isn't PQ, therefore build a transparent STARK VM. That's a multi-year cryptography research programme acquired as a consequence of a different decision, not chosen on its own merits. It may still be right. But it should be justified directly, because it's plausibly the largest single item in the plan.

The part that's pure waste

This is the one I'd fix first, and it's the reason TODO.md exists.

A meaningful fraction of current complexity is optionality being paid for in specification. The repo holds both economic models simultaneously — so MINT.md's issuance curves, REWARDS.md's five verifier roles and bond lots and reward pools, and DECENTRALIZATION.md's staking analysis all describe machinery that gets deleted entirely if the no-token branch wins (#313). Likewise REWARDS.md §6's whole compute-market apparatus exists for a plane that may not belong in consensus at all (#315).

That complexity isn't serving the product. It's serving indecision. Resolving those two issues doesn't just unblock work — it deletes documents. That's the cheapest complexity reduction available to you and it costs nothing but a decision.

The measurement that settles it objectively

Everything above is judgment. There's one non-subjective test, and it's #317: what does a conforming second client cost?

Bitcoin's validity rules fit in one person's head. Ethereum's take a team-year. If ActiveChain's v1.0 surface can't be independently implemented, then the complexity has exceeded what the design can carry — and it fails specifically at DECENTRALIZATION.md's headline property, cheap independent verification, which is only real if someone independent actually verifies. That number is the complexity budget. Right now it's unknown, which means the question "is this too complicated?" currently has no answer rather than a bad one.

What I'd actually say

The code is disciplined. The program isn't. Each piece is small, bounded, and provable; there are just too many pieces planned to land simultaneously, and one maintainer plus agents is constrained by review capacity more than throughput — formal methods help, but Lean models are themselves artifacts that need maintaining.

You've already said scope stays, and that's fine — three different budgets are in play and only one is permanent:

- the programme (everything you intend to build, ever) — no reason to cut
- v1.0 mandatory (#314) — sequencing, fully reversible, already being fixed
- the consensus surface — the only permanent one, because P-000 §9 means what goes in can never be reinterpreted out

The highest-leverage move is applying ARCHITECTURE_GUIDE.md §2's admission test to every plane, not just compute jobs. That test is already written and it's a good one. It's just never been run as an audit. Anything that fails it moves above consensus, stays in the programme, and stops costing you permanent complexity.

So: does it have to be this complicated? At the consensus layer, less than it currently is. In total, probably yes — but spread across a decade of versions rather than one genesis.
